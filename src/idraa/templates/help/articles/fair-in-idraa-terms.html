The FAIR model — concept spine

{% include "help/figures/factor_tree.html" %}

Idraa is built on FAIR (Factor Analysis of Information Risk), an Open FAIR™ standard for expressing cyber risk as a financial quantity. Every number the engine produces traces back to the three relationships below. Understanding them tells you exactly what you are estimating when you fill in a scenario form.

1. Risk = Loss Event Frequency × Loss Magnitude

How often does a harmful event occur, and how much does it cost each time? Those two quantities — Open FAIR nodes Loss Event Frequency (LEF) and Loss Magnitude (LM) — are the top-level factors. Risk is their product, sampled thousands of times across your input ranges by Monte Carlo simulation.

2. LEF = Threat Event Frequency × Vulnerability

Not every attempt causes a loss. Threat Event Frequency (TEF) counts how often a threat actor tries the attack per year. Vulnerability is the probability that any one attempt succeeds. Loss Event Frequency is their product: the expected number of successful harmful events per year.

3. Vulnerability = P(Threat Capability > Control Strength)

Formally, Vulnerability is the probability that a threat actor’s capability exceeds the asset’s resistance. In the scenario wizard you author the inherent Vulnerability — the baseline before any of your controls are applied. The controls you tick are modelled separately and reduce this probability in the simulation; entering a mentally-discounted figure would credit them twice. The Open FAIR nodes involved are Threat Capability (TC) and Control Strength / Resistance Strength (CS).

One terminology warning: this is not the vulnerability-scanner sense of the word. Security practitioners are trained to read “vulnerability” as a defect — an unpatched CVE, a weak configuration — but in FAIR the word names how likely an attempt is to succeed: always a probability, never a finding you can point at. A defect matters because it weakens resistance and so raises the chance an attempt succeeds; in Idraa that effect reaches the analysis through the controls you tick (patch state, for example, is a modelled control), not by folding control state into the inherent figure you author here. Jack Freund & Jack Jones adopt “susceptibility” as an official synonym for the FAIR sense (Measuring and Managing Information Risk, 2015, p. 5 n. 1) — partly because FAIR applies to risk domains beyond cyber, where the word is rarely used, and partly to head off exactly this confusion. The scenario wizard uses the same word when it asks for the asset’s inherent susceptibility.

4. LM = Primary Loss + Secondary Loss (per loss event)

Loss Magnitude is the total cost per loss event, not an annual figure. It splits into two Open FAIR nodes: Primary Loss (PL) — direct costs such as incident response, recovery, and ransom — and Secondary Loss (SL) — knock-on costs such as regulatory fines, lost business, and reputational damage. The engine annualises them by multiplying each simulated per-event magnitude by the simulated event count for that year.

ALE: the mean annual loss

Annualized Loss Expectancy (ALE) is the mean of the simulated annual-loss distribution — the long-run expected loss per year. It is a v3 view-model derivation (not itself a named Open FAIR node) computed from the LEF × LM product distribution. Because cyber-loss distributions are heavily right-skewed, the mean exceeds the median; ALE always refers to the mean, never the median. Use the Run & read analyses article for guidance on reading the full distribution rather than relying on ALE alone.

Monte Carlo simulation

Nobody knows the exact value of TEF, Vulnerability, PL, or SL. You enter a low–high range — the bounds you are 90% confident contain the true value (formally, the 5th and 95th percentiles of your belief distribution). The engine draws thousands of random samples from that range, computes Risk = LEF × LM for each draw, and collects the results into an annual-loss distribution. The result is not one number but a realistic spread: the typical year, a bad year, and the rare disaster.

The 5th and 95th percentile framing sets the shape of the fitted distribution — it is not a live hard min/max clip applied at sample time. By default, Threat Event Frequency and Loss Magnitude (Primary and Secondary Loss) inputs are fitted as a lognormal and then collapsed to a bounded PERT distribution for sampling, so under default settings your high estimate is the ceiling of the sampled values. Loss Magnitude entries you flag as catastrophic are the documented exception: they keep the heavy-tailed lognormal shape (rather than collapsing to a bounded PERT), bounded above at your organization’s capacity cap rather than at any single expert’s 95th-percentile judgement. Vulnerability is always a probability (0–100%) and uses the same capped-PERT approach, bounded to [0, 100%].

Lognormal authoring — the distribution assumption

Cyber loss and frequency quantities are right-skewed with a heavy upper tail, so Idraa elicits them as a lognormal: your low and high estimates are treated as the 5th and 95th percentiles of a lognormal belief distribution. This elicitation shape is well-supported in the calibration literature. Douglas Hubbard and Richard Seiersen note (in How to Measure Anything in Cybersecurity Risk, 2nd ed., 2023, ch. 6) that 90% credible intervals elicited as low–high estimates map naturally onto lognormal parameters via the 5th–95th percentile pair. Jack Jones and the FAIR Institute document the same elicitation assumption in the FAIR-CAM calibration framework. Neither source is a claim about the shape Idraa ultimately samples from — see below.

Idraa fits a right-skewed lognormal to your 5th/95th anchors and then collapses it to a bounded PERT for sampling — your high estimate is the ceiling of the sampled distribution. This is a deliberate choice over a runtime clip, which would instead dump the lognormal’s tail mass into an artificial spike sitting exactly at the cap. Loss Magnitude (Primary and Secondary Loss) entries flagged catastrophic are the exception: they keep the heavy-tailed lognormal instead of collapsing to PERT, bounded above only at your organization’s capacity cap rather than at any single expert’s 95th-percentile judgement — your high estimate is the 95th percentile of that belief distribution, not a ceiling. Because Primary and Secondary Loss are each bounded independently, a single loss event’s total is bounded by the sum of both caps, not by either cap alone.

Bayesian or frequentist?

The probabilities in FAIR — and therefore in Idraa — are subjective (Bayesian) probabilities: statements of how uncertain you are about a specific future outcome given what you know today, not long-run frequencies over repeated trials. That is why the wizard asks for calibrated 90% ranges rather than a single point value. Historical data still informs your ranges as evidence — incident counts are good grounding for a frequency estimate — but for a one-off outcome like “this organization suffers a major outage next year” there is no repeatable experiment, and so no well-defined long-run frequency. The FAIR literature (Freund & Jones, 2015) adopts this interpretation, and it is the same stance behind Hubbard & Seiersen’s calibrated-estimation method cited above.

A Bayesian interpretation does not mean Bayesian inference. The engine performs forward Monte Carlo propagation: it samples your input distributions and pushes the samples through the factor tree. There is no prior-times-likelihood step and no posterior — when new evidence arrives (an incident, a pentest finding, better industry data), you update the model by re-eliciting the input ranges, not by feeding observations to the engine. The engine never adjusts your estimates on its own.

One nuance the FAIR standard is careful about, and Idraa preserves: the model mixes genuine frequencies with genuine probabilities. Threat Event Frequency and Loss Event Frequency are rates — events per year, which can exceed 1 — while Vulnerability is a probability bounded to 0–100%. The two are kept semantically distinct throughout the engine; neither is converted into the other.

The one-line summary: FAIR uses subjective probabilities, elicited through calibrated estimation and propagated by Monte Carlo simulation — it does not perform Bayesian updating. Results are statements of belief conditional on your inputs, and they are as defensible as the estimates behind them — which is why the raw sample export exists, so you can verify the propagation yourself.
