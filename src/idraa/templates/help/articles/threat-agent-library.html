What a threat community is

A threat community is a group of threat agents who share enough characteristics that one profile describes them: who they are, what they want, how capable they are, and how they go about it. Every scenario in Idraa should name one; a scenario carried over from before this release without a clear match is flagged for review until you choose. The community gives the FAIR questions on the likelihood page their subject (“how often might cybercriminals try to…”), it is the vocabulary every analyst in your workspace shares, and it is the lens the dashboard and the PDF use to show where your modelled loss sits. Idraa ships nine canonical communities, browsable at Library → Threat communities: nation-state, cybercriminals, hacktivists, competitors, privileged insider, non-privileged insider, accidental insider, third party (a supplier’s own personnel acting through granted access), and opportunistic hackers.

The community does not change any computed figure. The simulation reads the frequency, vulnerability and loss ranges you author; the community is recorded beside them so that the same question is asked the same way every time, and so that reports can group by it.

The profile

Each community carries the same eight attributes, so two communities can be compared side by side and a scenario author can see at once which one fits:

  • Motive — what the community is after.
  • Primary intent — the effect it wants on your assets.
  • Sponsorship — who funds or directs it.
  • Preferred target characteristics — what makes an organisation attractive to it.
  • Preferred targets — the kinds of organisation and asset it goes after.
  • Capability — what it can do, relative to the general threat population.
  • Personal risk tolerance — how much exposure its members accept.
  • Collateral damage concern — how much it cares about harm beyond its objective.

Below the attributes, each profile states its counting rule (what counts as one threat event), a default frequency range and, for the malicious communities, a default capability band, with the reasoning and the sources behind each number. The accidental-insider community carries no capability band: an error is not a contest against the asset’s resistance.

The counting rule

Threat Event Frequency only means something if everyone counts the same thing. For the malicious communities a threat event is a serious, intentional act against one of your assets — an attempt to gain access, exfiltrate, extort, defraud, deface or disrupt. Scans, minor misbehaviour and errors are not counted. For the accidental insider a threat event is an error that, with nothing intervening, would leave the asset in a loss-capable condition: a misdirected disclosure, a misconfiguration that exposes it, a lost or mishandled record, a mistaken transaction. Only slips the person notices and corrects before the action takes effect are left out.

The rule is control-naive for every community: a malicious attempt counts whether or not your controls stop it, and an error counts even when a control blocks or catches it. This matters because Idraa’s control layer already routes prevention onto Threat Event Frequency (see the control model). If you counted only the attempts that got through, the control would be credited twice — once in your frequency estimate and once by the engine — and the modelled reduction would be overstated.

Scenarios created before this release that name the accidental insider were elicited under older question wording. Their numbers are unchanged; when you next re-estimate one, read the counting rule above before answering.

Landmarks, not inputs

Each profile shows a default range (landmark) for Threat Event Frequency, labelled 5th · most likely · 95th — the same convention every library entry and every wizard frequency range uses: the 5th and 95th percentiles are the bounds of the sampled distribution, and the middle value is its most-likely point. The range is an organisation-level base rate for a reference organisation (a mid-sized, sector-neutral company) across all of its assets. To use it, first adjust it up or down for your own organisation — your size, sector and profile make you more or less attractive than the reference — and then scope it down to the single asset and method your scenario describes. A landmark used unadjusted is almost always wrong: scoping to one asset and method lowers it, but the source’s event threshold (below) pulls the other way and usually dominates, and it is off in whichever direction your organisation differs from the reference. Every range recovered from an incident or loss statistic is also biased low, for the reasons its profile lists — an optimistic bias, not a safe one. The largest reason is the source: a landmark recovered from public-record loss events sits well below what a scenario counts, and the library’s own entries routinely place their most-likely frequency above the landmark’s 95th. A scenario frequency above the landmark is not, by itself, extreme.

Idraa calls these ranges landmarks and deliberately avoids the Bayesian name for a starting distribution. A starting distribution in that sense is something a model updates with evidence; Idraa has no such step. It runs a forward Monte Carlo on the ranges you author and never revises an estimate from data (see probability interpretation). A landmark is a place to start reasoning, visible on the profile page and nowhere in the calculation.

Where a landmark was recovered from an incident or loss statistic rather than from a count of attempts, the profile says so, and shows the conversion rate the curator divided by to get back to attempts. That rate is a curator convention describing a controlled world — roughly the fraction of attempts that become an incident or loss event once the controls in the asset’s path have acted. It leaves out the prevention that stops attempts earlier, one reason these ranges run low, and it exists only to recover the attempt rate. Do not reuse it as your scenario’s inherent Vulnerability: the Vulnerability you author is the chance an attempt succeeds against the asset before your controls, which is a different and generally larger number.

Privileged insiders and your controls

The privileged-insider community’s capability reflects not needing to defeat preventative controls: the account already has the rights an outsider would have to earn. The engine credits every linked control uniformly — it cannot tell that a particular control sits outside the insider’s path. So for a scenario assigned to this community, do not link preventative controls that the insider’s granted access bypasses, or the modelled reduction is overstated. Link the controls that still stand in the way: logging and monitoring, segregation of duties and approval checks the insider cannot satisfy alone, and the detective and response controls that act after the fact.

Split or lump?

Nine communities is a starting point, not a ceiling. Treat a group of threat agents as its own community when it differs from its neighbours in how often it acts against you, in its skillset, or in which of your controls are relevant to it; when it does not differ on any of those, lumping it with the nearest community loses nothing. Contractors and other non-employee affiliates with ordinary access belong with the non-privileged insider by default; move one to the privileged insider only when the role actually carries elevated rights, and to third party only when a supplier’s own people act through the access you granted their employer.

Where this comes from

The idea of a threat agent library with a fixed set of profiling attributes comes from the FAIR literature: Jack Jones and Jack Freund, Measuring and Managing Information Risk: A FAIR Approach, 2nd edition, chapter 4 (threat profiling), which in turn builds on Matthew Rosenquist’s Threat Agent Risk Assessment work at Intel. From that lineage Idraa adopts the attribute structure, the idea of a starter set of communities, the practice of publishing default ranges as starting points the analyst adjusts, the split-or-lump test, and the convention of expressing capability as a percentile rank. The control-naive counting rule is Idraa’s own; it follows from how Idraa’s control layer credits prevention. Every sentence of the profiles and every default range are Idraa’s own, with the sources and the derivation stated on each profile page.
